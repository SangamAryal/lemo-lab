# "A Journey to the Bottom of the Ocean" — script, draft 1

**Channel:** Beyond Everything. **Style:** our brand flat-vector look. Vertical 1080×1920, about 2 min 38 s, inside YouTube's 3-minute Shorts limit. **Voice:** Ryan (UK), the same as the last two films. A narration preview is in [narration-preview.mp3](narration-preview.mp3).

**Reference coverage:** every fact in the Insider Tech video ("This Incredible Animation Shows How Deep The Ocean Really Is", 2017) is in here. I took its full narration from the video's captions; it's listed at the bottom of this file. Where the 2017 video is out of date or slightly off, the script uses the corrected figure (see FACTS.md), and I added four newer facts: the deepest fish, the deepest octopus sighting, the beaked whale's breath-hold, and the people who've reached the bottom since 2019.

**The device:** one continuous dive. A cute yellow **submarine character** (round, one porthole eye area with a face, propeller tail) sinks down a tall water column, and the camera rides with it. A **depth gauge** at the top shows metres *and* feet, because the reference video's top comments complain it only used feet. The water darkens zone by zone: sunlit blue, twilight teal, midnight black, abyss, hadal. The sub's headlight switches on at 1,000 m. Things appear beside the sub at their real depths.

## Reference video narration (Insider Tech, 2017), taken from its captions
If you took the highest point on land and submerged it, you would still have more than a mile between you and the deepest point in the oceans. The oceans harbor 99% of all living space on Earth, and have enough water to fill a bathtub that's 685 miles long on each side. For scale, here's a human, and here's a blue whale, the largest animal on Earth. Blue whales usually hunt at depths of around 330 ft, within the well-lit zone of the ocean. Deeper down at 700 ft, the USS Triton became the first submarine to circumnavigate the Earth, in 1960. At 831 ft, we reach the deepest free dive in recorded history. Down here the pressure is 26 times greater than at the surface, which would crush most human lungs. But whales manage it, diving to a max depth of 1,640 ft, where they hunt giant squid. At 2,400 ft, we reach the danger zone for modern nuclear attack submarines; any deeper and the submarine's hull would implode. 2,722 ft down is where the tip of the world's tallest building, the Burj Khalifa, would reach. A little farther, at 3,280 ft, we're deep enough that sunlight can't reach us; we've now entered the midnight zone. Many animals down here can't see, like these eyeless shrimp at 7,500 ft, who thrive near scalding hot underwater volcanoes. At this depth temperatures are just a few degrees above freezing, but the waters around hydrothermal vents can heat up to 800 °F. 9,816 ft is the deepest any mammal has been recorded swimming: the Cuvier's beaked whale. But not even the Cuvier's beaked whale could explore the RMS Titanic, which rests at a staggering depth of 12,500 ft. The pressure is now 378 times greater than at the surface, yet you can still find sea life like the fangtooth, hagfish, and dumbo octopus, the deepest-living octopus on Earth. At 20,000 ft is the hadal zone, an area designated for the ocean's deepest trenches, like the Mariana Trench. If you tipped Mount Everest into the Mariana Trench, its summit would reach down to 29,029 ft. That still doesn't compare to the two deepest crewed missions in history. In 2012, director James Cameron descended to 35,756 ft for the Deepsea Challenger mission. But Cameron didn't quite break the record, which was set by oceanographer Jacques Piccard and Lieutenant Don Walsh in 1960. Piccard and Walsh descended to the lowest point on Earth, Challenger Deep, at a record 35,797 ft below the surface. Since then, scientists have sent half a dozen unmanned submersibles to explore Challenger Deep, including Kaiko, which collected over 350 species off the sea floor between 1995 and 2003. But scientists estimate there are potentially thousands of marine species we have yet to discover. Humans have explored an estimated 5 to 10% of Earth's oceans. We've only just begun to understand the deep, dark world that flows beneath us.
