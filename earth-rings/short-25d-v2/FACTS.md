# FACTS — What If Earth Had Rings?

| Claim | Check / source | Hedge |
|---|---|---|
| A ring would arc across the sky from horizon to horizon | Rings sit over the equator. From mid-latitudes they would look like an arch; from the equator, a thin line overhead; from near the poles, they would be hidden below the horizon. | "would" |
| Rings in sunlight make nights brighter | Ring particles outside Earth's shadow reflect sunlight. How bright depends on the material: ice is bright, rock is dark. | "would", "a lot" |
| Ring shadow falls on the winter hemisphere | Same geometry as Saturn's seasonal ring shadows. The shadow band sits over the tropics and subtropics of the winter half. | "a wide strip" (not "half the planet") |
| Ring debris threatens satellites crossing the ring | Any orbit that crosses the equatorial plane passes through it twice per orbit. The ISS, for example, is inclined 51.6°. | "any satellite that crossed it" |
| Rings don't last forever (Moon, collisions, drag) | Ring particles collide and spread out. The Moon and the Sun perturb them, and the innermost particles would drag down into the atmosphere. Saturn's own rings may be young and are slowly "raining" into Saturn (Cassini; O'Donoghue et al. 2018). | "some could fall" |
| Earth may have had a rocky ring about 466 million years ago | Tomkins, Martin & Cawood, "Evidence suggesting that Earth had a ring in the Ordovician", *Earth and Planetary Science Letters* 646 (2024). 21 impact craters from that time lie within about 30° of the old equator, which suggests a broken-up asteroid formed a ring. | "Some scientists think", "a rocky ring?" on screen |
| Its shadow may have helped cool the planet | The same paper proposes that the ring's shadow contributed to the Hirnantian ice age (about 445 Ma). This is a hypothesis, not settled. | "may have helped"; "maybe an ice age" on screen |
| Long before dinosaurs | Dinosaurs appear about 230 Ma; 466 Ma is in the Ordovician. ✓ | — |
