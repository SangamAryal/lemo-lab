# FACTS — The Moon Is Slowly Breaking Up With Us

| Claim | Source and wording |
|---|---|
| The Moon drifts about 3.8 cm farther each year | NASA (Eclipse web site, "Measuring the Moon's Distance"): "The Moon is receding from Earth at about 3.8 cm per year." https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/help/ApolloLaser.html |
| About as fast as fingernails grow | NASA statement (August 2020), quoted widely: Earth and the Moon are moving apart "at the rate that fingernails grow, or 1.5 inches (3.8 centimeters) per year". |
| Astronauts left mirrors; we still bounce lasers off them | NASA: Apollo 11 placed the first laser-ranging reflector in 1969 (100 fused-silica corner cubes); round-trip timing gives the distance to about 3 cm. Apollo 14 and 15 and two Soviet Lunokhod rovers also carry reflectors. Light's round trip at ~385,000 km is about 2.5 s. |
| Earth spins faster than the Moon orbits, so the tidal bulge runs ahead and tugs the Moon forward into a higher orbit; the Moon brakes Earth's spin | Universe Today, "Why is the Moon Leaving Us?"; NASA statement (tidal interaction). The standard tidal-acceleration mechanism. https://www.universetoday.com/112450/why-is-the-moon-leaving-us/ |
| Days lengthen by a couple of thousandths of a second per century | About 1.7–2.3 ms per century, from ancient eclipse records (Stephenson et al.). Cited in the NASA eclipse material and Universe Today. |
| The young Moon looked more than ten times bigger in the sky | Simulation (arXiv:1503.03041): early-Moon angular diameter about 6.25°, compared with about 0.5° today. NASA's Sarah Noble (Gravity Assist podcast): "the Moon was much closer and would have been much bigger and brighter in the sky." |
| A day lasted less than ten hours when the Moon formed | StarDate (University of Texas McDonald Observatory): Earth "was spinning much faster, with a day length of less than 10 hours"; impact models (Ćuk & Stewart) use 2–3 hour days. https://stardate.org/astro-guide/topic/earths-moon |
| 1.4 billion years ago a day was about 18.7 hours (not in narration; backup) | Meyers & Malinverno, PNAS 2018 (UW–Madison release): days "just over 18 hours". |
| In the age of dinosaurs (70 million years ago), a day was 23.5 hours and a year had 372 days | AGU press release on de Winter et al. 2020 (*Paleoceanography and Paleoclimatology*), from a rudist clam shell: "Earth rotated 372 times a year, compared to the current 365, and a day lasted only 23 and a half hours." https://news.agu.org/press-release/ancient-shell-shows-days-were-half-hour-shorter-70-million-years-ago/ |
| In about 600 million years the Moon will be too small to cover the Sun; no more total eclipses | Space.com quoting a NASA scientist: Earth "will have its last total solar eclipse in about 600 million years". Sky & Telescope (Jean Meeus): totality becomes on-and-off from about 620 million years and ends by about 1.2 billion years. https://www.space.com/37627-total-solar-eclipse-earth-moon-alignment-future.html |
| The Moon won't fully leave; the Sun intervenes first | Wikipedia, *Tidal locking*: Earth–Moon locking would take about 50 billion years (day ≈ 47 of today's days). The Sun becomes a red giant in about 5 billion years. Universe Today: the Sun will end it first. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_locking |
| We live in the window when the Moon fits the Sun | Sky & Telescope / Space.com: the Sun is ~400× wider than the Moon and ~400× farther, so they appear nearly the same size, a coincidence of our era. |

## Community questions this answers
- "Will the Moon eventually fly away?" is the most common question (forum threads, "I'm a Scientist", Universe Today comments). The answer is no: tidal locking or the Sun ends it first. That's the twist in line 10.
