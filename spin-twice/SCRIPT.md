**What changed from Sangam's draft, and why**
- **Fixed:** "a year would still have roughly 365 days" is wrong. Earth's orbit takes the same ~8,766 hours, so with
  12-hour days a year has **about 730 days**. (The draft's own next sentence, twice as many sunrises, already implies this.)
- **Made concrete:** "the equator would bulge more" → the oceans pour toward the equator (polar seas drain, equatorial
  coasts drown). "Gravity would feel weaker" → **about 1 % lighter at the equator**. "Weather more complicated" → winds
  split into more bands, like Jupiter, and less heat reaches the poles.
- **Made concrete:** "fighting your body's natural rhythm" → the body clock (~24 h) can't fit a 12-hour day, so you'd
  sleep through one night and stay up through the next.
- **Kept the order** (slow consequences first, the sudden spin-up as the payoff) and framed it as "that's if it sped up
  slowly. Overnight…", with the real number: the ground racing through the air at over 1,600 km/h at the equator.
- **Added** the good-news beat from the black hole formula: it can't happen, Earth's spin is slowing (the Moon's tides).
- **Cut** for length (the draft was ~270 words, ~90 s): the duplicate "less time to sleep" setup and "you'd probably
  wish for a little more time".

## Fact checks

| Line | Claim (as said or shown) | Basis | Hedge |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Earth spinning twice as fast | A thought experiment ("what if"). Today one rotation relative to the Sun takes 24 h (sidereal 23 h 56 min); twice as fast ≈ a 12-hour solar day (exactly ~11 h 58 min). | "What if" |
| 2 | A day lasts 12 hours; sunrise, sunset and midnight twice as often | Follows from line 1. | — |
| 4 | About 730 days in a year | The year is set by the orbit (365.25 days × 24 h ≈ 8,766 h), which doesn't change. 8,766 h ÷ 12 h ≈ 730 days. Sangam's draft said 365; corrected. | "about" |
| 5 | The body clock can't squeeze into 12 hours; you'd sleep through one night and stay up through the next | The human circadian clock runs at ~24.2 h and can only entrain to days within roughly ±1–2 h of that; 12-hour light–dark cycles are outside that range, so people would keep a ~24-hour rhythm (Czeisler et al., Science 1999; Duffy & Wright 2005, "Entrainment of the human circadian system by light"). | "can't squeeze" |
| 5a | Permanent jet lag, linked to poor sleep and health problems | Living against the body clock (shift work, chronic jet lag) is linked to poor sleep and to metabolic and heart problems (Scheer et al. 2009, PNAS; CDC/NIOSH on shift work). | "linked to" |
| 5b | Animals run on body clocks too; birds, bees and flowers time their day by the Sun | Circadian clocks are near-universal: birds' daily activity and migration timing, honeybees' time sense for foraging, flowers opening at set times of day (Linnaeus' flower clock). Their clocks run near 24 h, so a 12-hour day would put them out of step. | "thrown off" |
| 5c | Crops grow worse when their inner clock doesn't match the day; harvests could shrink | Plants whose circadian period matches the day-night cycle fix more carbon and grow faster than mismatched ones (Dodd et al. 2005, Science). | "could" |
| 5d | High tide about every six hours, not every twelve | Today Earth turns under the Moon's two tidal bulges once every ~24 h 50 min, so high tide comes every ~12 h 25 min. At twice the spin that becomes ~12.2 h, so high tide about every 6 h. | "about" |
| 6 | The faster spin flings the oceans toward the equator; polar seas drain; equatorial cities drown | The ocean surface follows the equipotential (geoid). The centrifugal potential difference between equator and pole, ½(ωR)², rises from ≈ 108 kJ/kg to ≈ 432 kJ/kg at 2× spin; divided by g that is ≈ 33 km more equator-to-pole height difference than today (less once the water's own gravity is included, still many km), far more than the ocean's ~3.7 km mean depth, so water drains from high latitudes and piles up at the equator (same physics as Esri / W. Fraczek's analysis of a stopped Earth, reversed). Rock would deform too, but far more slowly. | "kilometres deep" on screen, no exact number spoken |
| 7 | About 1 % lighter at the equator | Centrifugal acceleration at the equator today ω²R ≈ 0.034 m/s² (0.35 % of g). At 2× spin it is 4× that, ≈ 0.136 m/s² (1.4 % of g), so about 1 % lighter than today. | "about" |
| 8 | Winds bend harder and split into more bands, like Jupiter's stripes | The Coriolis effect doubles; climate models of faster-rotating planets show more, narrower jet streams and wind bands (Kaspi & Showman 2015, ApJ; Navarra & Boccaletti 2002). Jupiter's many bands come with its ~10-hour day. | "a bit like" |
| 9 | Less heat would reach the poles; tropics hotter, poles colder | Faster rotation weakens the equator-to-pole heat transport by large eddies, so the equator-to-pole temperature difference grows in those same models (Kaspi & Showman 2015). | "could" |
| 10 | Overnight, the air and oceans keep their old speed | Inertia: unattached air and water keep their momentum when the ground speeds up. | "Overnight" (sudden) |
| 11 | At the equator the ground races through the air at over 1,600 km/h, with giant ocean surges | Equatorial rotation speed today = 40,075 km ÷ 23.93 h ≈ 1,670 km/h. Doubling the spin makes the ground move ~1,670 km/h faster than the air and water still moving at the old speed. | "over" |
| 12 | It can't happen | No known natural process could double Earth's spin; it would need an energy input of about 6.4 × 10²⁹ J (3× today's rotational energy of 2.1 × 10²⁹ J). | — |
| 12 | Earth's spin is slowing down, thanks to the Moon | Tidal friction from the Moon lengthens the day by ~2.3 ms per century; observed net ≈ 1.8 ms per century (Stephenson et al. 2016, Proc. R. Soc. A). | "very slowly"; on-screen "~2 ms" |

Not claimed: a number of deaths, earthquakes or volcanoes, how fast rock would reshape, or exact storm strengths.
