# What Is Beyond the Edge of the Observable Universe?

Narrator: en-GB-RyanNeural +5%. Length about 62 s. User script with three edits (radio-detected light and glowing gas, the "own bubble" line, the "far more than our bubble" line).

The oldest light we can detect left a glowing cloud of gas 13.8 billion years ago.
But that gas is now 46 billion light-years away.
How? Space kept stretching while the light was on its way to us.
That's the edge of the observable universe: a bubble 93 billion light-years wide, with us in the middle.
Not because we're special. Anyone, anywhere, is in the middle of their own bubble.
But it's not a wall. It's just how far light has had time to travel.
Past it, space keeps going. More galaxies. More stars. Maybe far more than everything inside our bubble.
And we'll never see most of them.
Some are moving away so fast that the light they send today will never reach us. Not in a billion years. Not ever.
So does the universe go on forever? Nobody knows.
It might be infinite.
Or it might curve back on itself like Earth's surface: finite, but with no edge to fall off.
Either way, every star you've ever seen is inside one bubble.
And that bubble might be a tiny speck of the whole thing.

## Fact checks
- Oldest detectable light: the cosmic microwave background, emitted about 13.8 billion years ago by hot gas (about 380,000 years after the Big Bang).
- That gas is now about 46 billion light-years away; the observable universe is about 93 billion light-years across.
- Every observer has their own observable bubble.
- Light emitted today from beyond the cosmic event horizon (about 16 billion light-years) never reaches us, assuming dark energy behaves as it does now.
- Infinite or finite-without-edge are both open possibilities.
